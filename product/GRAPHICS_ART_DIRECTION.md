# Bearing — Graphics & Art Direction Standard

**Status:** active editorial-production standard.

This document exists because repeated user corrections have established that technically valid graphics are not enough. Bearing graphics must be intentionally art-directed, editorially useful, and visually coherent.

The standard applies to original explanatory graphics and motion design. It does not turn graphics into documentary evidence.

## The split

Bearing should keep four distinct responsibilities:

1. **Visual Intelligence** — what documentary imagery actually proves, what it cannot prove, and what is visually missing.
2. **Graphics Intelligence** — what information genuinely requires graphical explanation and exactly what the graphic must communicate.
3. **Art Direction / Motion Design** — how the approved graphic should be composed, prioritized, animated, paced and integrated into the story.
4. **Renderer** — deterministic execution of the approved specification.

Art Direction may begin as a bounded stage using the same underlying model. Do not create a new autonomous service merely to satisfy the diagram.

## A graphic needs a reason to exist

Before rendering, answer:

**What will the viewer understand from this graphic that they would not understand as well from the available documentary imagery and narration alone?**

Good reasons include:
- relationship,
- sequence,
- comparison,
- geography,
- scale,
- process,
- change over time,
- cause and effect,
- a small set of precise values that need visual comparison.

Bad reasons include:
- the script contains a noun,
- a sentence can be turned into a card,
- the frame feels empty,
- motion would look polished,
- the same words can be repeated onscreen.

If documentary imagery communicates the idea better, use the imagery.

## Composition

- One primary idea per visual beat.
- Use the full 16:9 frame confidently.
- Avoid cramped cards, dashboards, UI panels and collections of little boxes unless the story itself requires a matrix.
- Establish the hierarchy immediately: the viewer should know where to look first.
- Fewer, larger elements are usually stronger than many small ones.
- Do not fill every region of the frame merely because space exists.
- Maintain intentional negative space.
- Preserve safe areas and readable margins.
- Avoid persistent decorative chrome around original graphics.

A finished frame should look **authored**, not like structured data was poured into a component.

## Typography and copy

- On-screen copy should be substantially shorter than narration.
- Do not transcribe the script into graphics.
- Prefer one clear headline or claim plus only the labels required to understand the visual.
- Break complex explanations into visual beats instead of shrinking type.
- Use typography to establish hierarchy, not decoration.
- Keep type sizes, spacing and alignment consistent across the piece.
- Do not use tiny source strips, eyebrows, footers or metadata as permanent visual furniture.
- Source attribution must be available and legible without becoming the composition.

## Motion

Motion has a job.

Use motion to:
- direct attention,
- reveal information in reading order,
- establish a relationship,
- show change,
- clarify cause and effect,
- move from one meaningful state to another.

Do not:
- animate continuously to imply polish,
- make everything float, pulse, glow or drift,
- move elements after the viewer already knows where to look,
- use motion that competes with narration,
- rush a graphic because the script is moving.

Build information in natural reading order, then **hold it long enough to understand**.

A hold is not dead air when the picture is still doing useful cognitive work.

Prefer purposeful cuts and authored transitions. Cross dissolves and generic easing should be editorial choices, not defaults.

## Visual hierarchy

Every graphic should define:

- **primary** — the one thing the viewer must understand,
- **secondary** — the context required to understand the primary idea,
- **tertiary** — optional detail that can be omitted if it weakens clarity.

If everything is emphasized, nothing is emphasized.

Color, scale, placement and motion should reinforce the same hierarchy rather than creating competing signals.

## Bearing aesthetic

Bearing should feel calm, precise, contemporary and editorial.

Avoid generic AI aesthetics:
- decorative gradients with no informational purpose,
- excessive glow,
- gratuitous particles,
- ornamental geometry,
- cyber/neon styling,
- glassmorphism for its own sake,
- random accent colors,
- template-like “explainer cards,”
- constant micro-animation.

A visually rich treatment is welcome when the subject earns it. Style must serve understanding.

## Documentary versus synthetic

- Synthetic or generated imagery must never masquerade as documentary evidence.
- Generic file imagery must not imply that it depicts the current event when it does not.
- Graphics should make explanatory abstraction obvious through design.
- Visual Intelligence owns the evidence boundary; Art Direction may not override it for aesthetics.

## Integration with the story

Graphics are part of the edit, not inserts dropped onto narration.

The Producer, Journalist, Visual Intelligence and Graphics Intelligence should agree on:
- where a graphic begins,
- what the narration is doing while it is onscreen,
- when the viewer has received enough information,
- what the next picture should accomplish.

Do not make every sentence a new scene.

A strong piece should alternate imagery and explanatory graphics according to what the story needs.

## Art Direction review

Before render, Art Direction should be allowed to:
- simplify,
- remove redundant copy,
- merge or split visual beats,
- change layout,
- change reveal order,
- change animation approach,
- reject a graphic that should be documentary imagery instead,
- send an unclear information request back to Graphics Intelligence.

Art Direction may **not**:
- change facts,
- change the editorial thesis,
- invent data,
- remove required qualifications,
- convert synthetic imagery into evidence,
- silently change source attribution.

Record material Art Direction changes so the experiment can determine whether this deserves a permanent newsroom responsibility.

## Standards / Final Edit acceptance

Standards should reject a technically valid graphic when it is:

- visually weak,
- redundant,
- overly textual,
- poorly composed,
- too dense,
- too fast to understand,
- inconsistent with the narration,
- unsupported,
- misleading,
- aesthetically generic in a way that harms the piece,
- or simply unnecessary.

Rendering successfully is not acceptance.

## Evaluation

Inspect the **encoded output**, not merely the scene specification or renderer code.

For each finished story:
- watch the complete moving piece,
- inspect representative encoded frames at full size,
- check reading time,
- check the first-look hierarchy,
- check whether motion has a purpose,
- check whether the picture and narration reinforce each other,
- ask whether any graphic could be removed without losing understanding.

Repeated user corrections should be folded back into this standard as durable principles. Do not require the user to keep giving the same art-direction notes story after story.
